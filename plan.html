    The Plan

    About the Plan

    The Maui Nui Landscape Conservation Plan (MNLCP) is the Hawaiʻi Division of Forestry and Wildlife's (DOFAW) roadmap for conserving native plants and animals across Maui, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, and Kahoʻolawe. It brings together where to manage, what to do, and when, for all of Maui Nui's at-risk species at once. It is meant to be used and updated, not shelved.

      Purpose and need

      The MNLCP has three complementary objectives: prevent the extinction of the most at-risk native plants and animals, restore all at-risk species to healthy, self-sustaining populations, and restore Maui Nui's native ecosystems so they provide habitat for at-risk species and other native species.plan p.1, lines 344-348

      Maui Nui spans high volcanic peaks; mesic, wet, and dry forests; subalpine shrublands; wetlands; streams; and coastal dunes. These habitats sustain hundreds of rare, threatened, and endangered species, most of which occur nowhere else on Earth.plan p.1, lines 351-355

      The plan's analysis covers all lands. Where possible, it focuses conservation actions on lands already managed or set aside for conservation, or covered by conservation agreements with private landowners.plan p.1, lines 356-358

      The challenge in depth

      A global crisis, felt most on islands

      Global biodiversity is being lost at rates unprecedented in human history. Island ecosystems are among the most vulnerable, and the Hawaiian Islands show how severe the crisis can be.plan p.1, lines 363-366 Hundreds of native Hawaiian species have already gone extinct, and hundreds more are at risk. Maui Nui alone has 442plan p.1, line 371 native plant and animal species that experts consider at risk of extinction in the near future. Their loss would erode not only biological diversity but also cultural heritage, ecosystem function, and economic well-being.plan p.1-2, lines 369-379

      How Hawaiʻi got here

      The Hawaiian Islands lie roughly 2,100 milesplan p.2, line 381 from the nearest continent. Long isolation produced remarkable endemism: about 90 percentplan p.2, line 386 of terrestrial species are found nowhere else.plan p.2, lines 380-387

      • Polynesian settlement, around a thousand years ago, brought agriculture, non-native species, and fire, and began the decline of native lowland ecosystems, including the loss of almost all of Hawaiʻi's flightless birds.plan p.2, lines 387-391
      • After Western contact in the late eighteenth century, ranching, plantation agriculture, commercial forestry, and urban development destroyed native forests, drained wetlands, diverted streams, and introduced aggressive invasive species and predators. Fire increased, erosion increased, and native habitats became fragmented.plan p.2, lines 392-397

      Threats now act at every scale: whole ecosystems (ungulates degrading wet forests, sea level rise flooding coastal habitats), whole taxonomic groups (predatory snails on native snails, avian malaria on forest birds), and single populations (wind turbines killing bats, housing built on rare plant habitat).plan p.2, lines 398-404

      With less than 0.2 percent of the land area of the United States, the Hawaiian Islands account for 29 percent of the nation's imperiled species.plan p.2, lines 406-408 (USFWS 2026)

      Many native species survive only in small, fragmented populations, often in high-elevation refuges or intensively managed, fenced areas. More than 60 percentplan p.2, line 410 of Hawaiʻi's native ecosystems have been destroyed or highly degraded. What remains depends largely on sustained, active management.plan p.2-3, lines 408-417

      Effort is growing, but not fast enough

      Conservation capacity in Hawaiʻi has grown, with more funding, more protected land, and more intensive management. Yet many species continue to decline, including hundreds with fewer than 50plan p.3, lines 426-427 individuals left in the wild. Current approaches are not keeping pace with the scale, complexity, and urgency of the crisis.plan p.3, lines 423-429

      Why existing plans are not enough

      Hawaiʻi has many high-quality plans: species recovery plans, the State Wildlife Action Plan, site management plans, and broader strategies. They provide essential scientific guidance, but they are not integrated at the landscape or management level.plan p.3, lines 430-434

      Managers must reconcile dozens of documents, each with different assumptions, scales, and objectives, on limited budgets and staff. This makes it hard to see multi-species opportunities, hard to prioritize, and slows progress toward preventing extinctions and restoring ecosystems.plan p.3, lines 434-439

      Even a relatively small reserve such as the Makawao Forest Reserve has habitat for many at-risk species. Until now, no planning framework could tell managers which of those species to recover there, how many individuals are needed, which areas matter most, or which combination of actions would be most cost-effective.plan p.3, lines 440-447

      The real challenge is not only that 442plan p.3, line 448 species are at risk. It is deciding where across the landscape each species should be recovered so that the needs of all species are met together. Every planning unit differs in habitat quality, climate resilience, species present, and how much it adds to recovery. Some help only a few species; others can advance recovery for dozens. The goal is not the best site for each species on its own, but a complementary network of places that together meets recovery objectives for all species.plan p.3-4, lines 448-458

      Working that out by hand, from dozens of recovery plans, for all 442plan p.4, line 461 species, is not feasible without an integrated analytical framework.plan p.4, lines 459-463

      What the MNLCP changes

      Led by DOFAW Maui with a broad coalition of public and private agencies, organizations, landowners, and technical experts, the MNLCP combines three things: spatial prioritization (where to focus land management), action prioritization (which conservation actions matter most), and ecosystem management that benefits all Maui Nui biodiversity.plan p.4, lines 464-470

      For the first time, this gives Maui Nui an islands-wide, landscape-scale roadmap showing where conservation can most effectively prevent extinctions, recover species, and restore ecosystems. Instead of working species by species or site by site, the plan sets population-level recovery objectives for all at-risk species in a spatially explicit, climate-resilient plan.plan p.4, lines 472-481

      Of the 442plan p.4, line 482 species, 202plan p.4, line 483 are federally or state-listed. The other 240plan p.4, line 483 have extremely small populations, very restricted ranges, expected harm from climate change, or declining trends. Recovering them takes coordinated actions: excluding non-native ungulates such as goats, deer, and cattle; weed control; predator and pest management; habitat restoration; fire prevention; disease mitigation; and in some cases ex situ propagation (growing or rearing outside the wild) and reintroduction.plan p.4-5, lines 482-495

      Applications and use

      The MNLCP is both a decision-support framework and a practical roadmap for implementation.plan p.5, lines 498-499 It helps land managers:

      • Prioritize actions based on effectiveness and efficiency
      • Estimate and plan for costs across species and sites
      • Identify lands that should be managed under long-term protection
      • Design integrated work plans that deliver several conservation benefits at one site
      • Coordinate actions across jurisdictions using shared dataplan p.5, lines 501-508

      For the wider conservation community, it sets out the full set of actions needed to prevent extinctions and recover all at-risk species in Maui Nui. It also supports work with landowners and communities, informs policy and planning, justifies investment, identifies research needs, and provides accountability through performance metrics and adaptive management.plan p.5, lines 509-519

      The MNLCP reframes the biodiversity crisis not as an insurmountable problem, but as a finite and solvable challenge.plan p.5, lines 512-515

      A path forward

      The plan's overarching goals are to prevent further extinctions, recover species now at risk, and stabilize ecosystems so they keep providing habitat for all native plants and animals, along with public benefits: clean water, food security, cultural value, and natural beauty.plan p.5, lines 522-526 Hawaiʻi's native species and habitats are linked to the state's ecological health, biocultural heritage, and economic prosperity. They form the foundation of cultural practices, natural resource management, and local economies.plan p.5-6, lines 526-531

      Three integrated goals

      Taken together, these goals protect the full breadth of Maui Nui's biodiversity:plan p.7, lines 550-551

      1. Prevent extinctions.
      2. Restore all currently at-risk species to healthy, self-sustaining population levels.
      3. Protect all remaining native ecosystems and restore degraded systems to provide enough habitat for all native Maui Nui biodiversity.plan p.7, lines 553-557

      Planning frameworks and approaches

      The MNLCP is an initial application of Integrated Multi-Scale Conservation Planning (IMCP). IMCP brings species recovery planning, spatial prioritization, threat-based action prioritization, ecosystem restoration planning, and adaptive implementation into one decision-support system that works across spatial, ecological, and management scales.plan p.7, lines 561-568

      Teams of experts on Hawaiian plants, animals, ecosystems, and land management guided the plan. They were supported by analytic teams of research scientists from USGS, USFWS, UH Mānoa, UH Hilo, and the Bishop Museum.plan p.7, lines 569-574

      The building blocks, in order:

      1. Recovery targets. Where possible, each at-risk species has a population-level recovery target based on abundance, distribution across islands and mountains, and viability, drawn from existing recovery plans and supplemented by expert input.plan p.7, lines 580-583
      2. Spatial context. Species distribution modeling, known occurrences, ecosystem integrity maps, published literature, and expert knowledge show where recovery can happen.plan p.8, lines 586-588
      3. Recovery footprints. Some taxonomic groups used expert knowledge to map the habitat needed for recovery directly. Others used spatial prioritization algorithms plus expert review to balance habitat needs with accessibility, ongoing management, climate resilience, and fire risk.plan p.8, lines 588-595
      4. Ecosystem planning identifies the ecosystems most critical to long-term biodiversity.plan p.8, lines 596-598
      5. Decision-support tools (spatial data layers, priority rankings, and threat-cost matrices) turn analysis into annual work plans, budgets, and interagency coordination.plan p.8, lines 598-600
      6. Monitoring and adaptive management provide feedback so the plan can evolve, with metrics for species recovery, threat reduction, and ecosystem condition.plan p.8, lines 601-603

      Procedural methods

      A project of this scope does not suit a species-by-species or one-size-fits-all approach. Because nothing of this scale and complexity had been attempted before, the plan uses a variety of methods, including several new or highly modified approaches.plan p.8, lines 606-611 The Approach page describes them.

      A 20-year horizon

      The MNLCP is large and complex, and it is designed for a 20-yearplan p.29, lines 1332-1333 implementation period. Its strategies contain many actions that must be carried out across large areas, so it cannot be done everywhere at once.plan p.29, lines 1332-1335 To help, species experts recommended an implementation sequence for each taxonomic group, and the plan identifies species facing imminent extinction that need action first.plan p.29, lines 1337-1341

      At-risk species are those likely to become extinct within the next 20 yearsplan p.16, lines 869-871 without significant management intervention, and the action prioritization measures success as the chance of viable, self-sustaining populations over the same 20-yearplan p.vii, line 254 period.

      A living plan

      The MNLCP is not meant to be a one-time, static document. It is a flexible, manager-driven, expert-informed decision-support system that helps land and species managers coordinate actions, identify priority areas, recommend implementable actions, set performance metrics, and build a foundation for adaptive management, budgeting, staffing, and partnership development.plan p.6, lines 532-537

      Together with the Adaptive Conservation Planner, the plan is a living system that managers can update as circumstances and funding change, as opportunities arise, and as new information comes in. It supports both long-range planning and annual implementation decisions.plan p.30, lines 1375-1379

      The MNLCP is grounded in science, informed by place, and driven by a shared commitment to ensure that Hawaiʻi's native species and ecosystems not only persist, but thrive once again.plan p.6, lines 539-542

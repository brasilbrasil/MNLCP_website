## Lead
The Maui Nui Landscape Conservation Plan (MNLCP) is built to be used. It brings together four answers that managers and field teams need: who needs management, where to focus, what needs to be done, and when. <!-- src: plan p.29, lines 1354-1361 --> This page walks through what the plan provides and how its products fit together on the ground.

## Key numbers
- **163** | species that need emergency action to prevent extinction within five years <!-- src: plan p.21, Table 2 line 1058 -->
- **27** | conservation strategies assessed: 17 single-focus and 10 combinations <!-- src: plan p.22, lines 1089-1091 -->
- **2** | combination strategies, S25 and S27, that together give the best outcomes for all eight groups <!-- src: plan p.23, lines 1116-1120 -->
- **50%** | minimum target probability of persistence over 20 years for each species group <!-- src: plan p.23, lines 1110-1114 -->
- **20** | year implementation period <!-- src: plan p.29, lines 1332-1333 -->

## What the MNLCP provides
The plan gives managers three kinds of guidance. <!-- src: plan p.20, lines 1006-1018 -->

1. **Emergency actions to prevent imminent extinctions.** These are mapped where possible. <!-- src: plan p.20, lines 1007-1008 -->
2. **Recovery actions** to bring at-risk species to healthy, self-sustaining population levels. Where possible, the plan maps both the habitat each species needs and the actions to take there. Species that lack the distribution or other information needed for mapping are targeted for surveys and studies. Their results will feed updates to the MNLCP. The aim is to identify habitat needs and conservation strategies for all 442 at-risk species. <!-- src: plan p.20, lines 1009-1016 -->
3. **Ecosystem conservation** assessments and actions, and the key areas that represent protection needs and conservation opportunities. <!-- src: plan p.20, lines 1017-1018 -->

## Emergency actions to prevent extinction
Species experts identified emergency actions or programs to prevent imminent extinctions in Forest birds, Plants, Terrestrial snails, Terrestrial arthropods, and Aquatic invertebrates. <!-- src: plan p.20, lines 1022-1024 --> Managing species in the wild (in situ) is usually preferred. But in situ work alone may not prevent extinction when the threat is a hard-to-detect invasive species, disease, extremely low numbers, or events that cannot be controlled, like climate change. <!-- src: plan p.20, lines 1024-1026 -->

Some emergency actions are land management in specific places. Others are research, or are not yet tied to particular sites. <!-- src: plan p.20, lines 1028-1030 --> Each [taxonomic group page](groups.html) gives more detail. <!-- src: plan p.20, lines 1030-1031 -->

## Where to work: habitat and spatial needs
The MNLCP recovery footprints identify the planning units (PUs, mapping cells of about 2 km²) that contain existing or restorable habitat at-risk species need to reach their recovery goals. <!-- src: plan p.21, lines 1063-1064; PU size p.vi line 228 --> Priority footprints pick out the most important areas to manage in the near term. Other areas follow in mid-term or later work. <!-- src: plan p.21, lines 1065-1067 -->

Combined across all eight taxonomic groups, the recovery footprint covers a significant portion of Maui Nui. It holds the habitat needed to recover, or significantly advance the recovery of, all 442 at-risk species except those still lacking distribution or habitat information. <!-- src: plan p.21, lines 1068-1071 -->

[figure: Combined recovery footprint for all taxonomic groups (green) with the priority areas for all groups (yellow) | suggested source: plan PDF p.22 Figure 2 | alt: Map of Maui, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi and Kahoʻolawe showing the combined recovery footprint in green and priority areas in yellow]

Terrestrial snails are the exception. The snail team determined that all 49 at-risk snails face imminent extinction. For now, the plan prioritizes preventing those losses with a number of snail exclosures (kīpuka kāhuli) rather than broader habitat recovery, which is not yet possible. Critical recovery work continues, especially research on controlling invasive predators and building supporting infrastructure. <!-- src: plan p.21, lines 1071-1079 -->

## What to do: conservation actions
The action prioritization effort (Priority Threat Management, or PTM, led by UH Mānoa) assessed the expected benefits of 27 conservation strategies: 17 single-focus strategies and 10 combinations of them (S18-S27). Each strategy was assessed for each species group. <!-- src: plan p.22, lines 1089-1096; team lead p.v lines 150-152 --> The analysis covered all eight MNLCP groups. It also split some groups into finer subgroups: 6 for Plants, 3 for Terrestrial arthropods, and 3 for Aquatic invertebrates. <!-- src: plan p.22, lines 1100-1103 -->

### The goal: a 50 percent chance of persistence
Persistence means the probability that a species group has viable, self-sustaining populations over 20 years. For many Hawaiian species this is hard to reach. The MNLCP's target is at least a 50 percent probability of persistence for each species group. <!-- src: plan p.23, lines 1110-1114 -->

No single strategy reached 50 percent for every group. Implementing the two best-performing combination strategies together gave the best outcomes for all eight groups: <!-- src: plan p.23, lines 1116-1120 -->

- **S25**, a suite focused on Plants and invertebrates, and
- **S27**, a suite focused on birds and the bat.
<!-- src: plan p.23-24, lines 1118-1119, 1133-1135 -->

S25 and S27 are the only combinations that include future ungulate fencing (S3), terrestrial habitat management (S5), and wetland management (S6). Choosing both means all 17 single-focus strategies get implemented. <!-- src: plan p.24, lines 1122-1129 -->

### What the results show
If every action in S25 and S27 were carried out and worked, every group could reach a 50 percent likelihood of persistence. Species experts judged some actions less feasible, so their expected benefits were reduced to reflect that. <!-- src: plan p.24, lines 1161-1168 --> Overall:

- Taking no action leaves most species groups with a very low probability of persistence.
- Current actions help, but only a little for many groups. They help Seabirds and Waterbirds most.
- Feasible actions significantly improve persistence for each species group.
- Forest birds have the lowest persistence today, even with current efforts, but also the most to gain from conservation action.
- Plants are the group least likely to reach 50 percent persistence.
<!-- src: plan p.24-25, lines 1170-1186 -->

## When to act: sequencing
The Taxonomic Teams also looked at how best to carry out the individual actions in S25 and S27. Order matters, because some actions give more benefit than others and some must be finished before others can start. <!-- src: plan p.25, lines 1196-1201 --> Before a key reintroduction, for example, a team may need to:

1. identify key threats, such as diseases or ungulates, and how to control them (and develop control methods if none exist)
2. put in fire breaks, if needed
3. collect seeds for restoration
4. fence the area
5. remove ecosystem threats like ungulates
6. remove weeds
7. propagate the species for reintroduction
8. reestablish and restore a native community, which could take years
9. provide short-term irrigation or threat control
10. reintroduce the species
<!-- src: plan p.25-26, lines 1201-1215 -->

Aquatic systems follow similar steps, such as restoring stream flows, removing invasive predators, installing predator barriers, and captive rearing before a translocation. <!-- src: plan p.26, lines 1216-1218 -->

As a general rule, start with the actions that give the greatest conservation benefit. But there are good reasons to adjust the order: <!-- src: plan p.29, lines 1343-1344 -->

- **Synergy.** Some actions do much more together than alone. Ungulate fencing paired with weed control delivers considerably more benefit than either alone. <!-- src: plan p.29, lines 1344-1347 -->
- **Timing.** Habitat restoration may have to come first to set the stage for a later reintroduction. <!-- src: plan p.29, lines 1347-1349 -->
- **Readiness and cost.** A highly cost-effective, medium-priority action that is ready now may be worth doing before a higher-benefit action that will not be field-ready for several years. <!-- src: plan p.29, lines 1349-1352 -->

Recommended sequences for each group are on the [taxonomic group pages](groups.html). <!-- src: plan p.26, lines 1220-1222 -->

## How the products fit together
Maui Nui's 442 at-risk species are not all equally imperiled. Some are down to a single individual that could die today. Others number in the thousands and may have a decade or more. Every one needs action, and the MNLCP recommends actions for all of them, but the urgency varies widely. <!-- src: plan p.26, lines 1227-1232 --> For species not facing imminent extinction, the plan works at the planning-unit, population, and ecosystem levels to identify the habitat and actions that will advance their recovery over the life of the plan. <!-- src: plan p.26, lines 1234-1237 -->

### Levels of conservation footprint
Recovery footprints were developed by the spatial prioritization team (led by USGS) together with the Taxonomic Teams. They show the areas likely to support the number of populations needed for recovery. <!-- src: plan p.26, lines 1239-1241; team lead p.v line 150 --> Within them, priority footprints flag the most important areas. For some groups, even the priority footprint is too large to manage all at once, so it was narrowed further into near-term work objectives or special actions. <!-- src: plan p.26-27, lines 1241-1248 --> Each group has spatial habitat maps at these levels:

- **Full recovery footprint:** provides enough habitat for recovery.
- **Priority recovery footprint:** the most important recovery areas.
- **Initial implementation footprint:** suggested starting points for conservation work.
<!-- src: plan p.27, lines 1248-1254 -->

:::details Worked example: narrowing the plant footprint
The plant recovery footprint covers 317 planning units. The plant priority footprint covers 155 planning units and holds habitat for three populations of each species. <!-- src: plan p.27, lines 1256-1259 -->

The Adaptive Conservation Planner (ACP) was then used to narrow the plant priority footprint in three steps: <!-- src: plan p.27, lines 1261-1262 -->

1. From all 195 at-risk Plants with habitat data to the 101 PEPP species (Plants facing imminent extinction) with habitat data.
2. Then to PEPP species with three or fewer known occupied planning units.
3. Finally, to PEPP species with a single known occupied planning unit.
<!-- src: plan p.27, lines 1264-1270 -->

How far to narrow depends on how urgently action is needed and what resources are available. ## Fine-scale implementation
In most cases, the MNLCP maps habitat and actions at the planning-unit level. Field teams decide the exact sites within each planning unit. <!-- src: plan p.29, lines 1364-1366 --> The MNLCP also provides implementation tools for this finer "within PU" planning. The Adaptive Conservation Planner (ACP) will be able to show the location of species occurrences, quality habitat, potential recovery sites, where climate seems to be changing fastest, and where certain microhabitats may be found within a planning unit. <!-- src: plan p.29-30, lines 1366-1373 -->

Used together with the ACP, the MNLCP is meant to be a living system. Managers can update or change it as circumstances, funding, opportunities, and information change. It supports both long-range planning and yearly implementation decisions. <!-- src: plan p.30, lines 1375-1380 -->

## How a manager would use the MNLCP
This walk-through puts the plan's guidance in order. It follows the steps Chapter 5 of the plan describes.

1. **Start with who needs help.** Check the at-risk species list for the species on the lands you manage, and note which face imminent extinction. The at-risk lists answer "who needs management". <!-- src: plan p.29, lines 1355-1356 --> [Browse the species explorer](species.html)
2. **Act on species facing imminent extinction.** Use the emergency actions for each group (Table 2 above). Every species facing imminent extinction receives actions to prevent its extinction, whatever its recovery or survey category. Knowing which species face imminent extinction is part of deciding "when actions need to be done". <!-- src: plan p.16-17, lines 893-896; p.20-21, Table 2; p.29, lines 1358-1359 -->
3. **Find where to work.** Look at the recovery footprint for each group, which answers "where to focus management". Use the priority footprint for near-term work and the initial implementation footprint as a starting point. <!-- src: plan p.21, lines 1063-1067; p.27, lines 1248-1254 -->
4. **Narrow the area to fit your urgency and resources.** If even the priority footprint is too large to manage at once, narrow it further, as the plant example shows. <!-- src: plan p.26-27, lines 1243-1248, 1272-1274 -->
5. **Choose what to do.** Draw on the actions in strategies S25 and S27 from the Priority Threat Management analysis, the "what needs to be done" part of the plan. <!-- src: plan p.28-29, lines 1323-1330 -->
6. **Decide when.** Follow the recommended sequence for each group. Generally start with the highest-benefit actions. Adjust for synergies, for steps that must come first, and for cost-effective actions that are ready now. <!-- src: plan p.29, lines 1337-1352 -->
7. **Plan within planning units.** Use the Conservation Planner's finer-scale information to site actions in the field. <!-- src: plan p.29-30, lines 1363-1373 -->
8. **Fill data gaps.** For species without recovery footprints, support area surveys and species-specific surveys. Their results will be added to the MNLCP. <!-- src: plan p.18, lines 959-961; p.20, lines 1012-1015 -->
9. **Update as you go.** Treat the MNLCP as a living system. Revisit priorities as funding, circumstances, opportunities, and information change, both for long-range planning and for yearly implementation decisions. <!-- src: plan p.30, lines 1375-1380 -->

> **Callout:** It is not possible to carry out a 20-year plan everywhere and all at once. Sequencing, together with knowing which species face imminent extinction, helps managers decide when each action needs to happen. <!-- src: plan p.29, lines 1332-1341 -->

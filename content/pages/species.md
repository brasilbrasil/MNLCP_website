## Lead
The first step in conservation planning is knowing which species are at risk. <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 860-861 --> The Maui Nui Landscape Conservation Plan (MNLCP) identifies 442 native birds, plants, invertebrates, and a bat that are likely to go extinct within the next 20 years without significant management. <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 869-870, 874-875 --> For each one, the plan sets out what is needed to prevent its extinction and, wherever the information allows, where and how to recover it.

## Key numbers
- **442** | native species at risk of extinction across Maui Nui <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 874-875; p.19 Table 1 line 999 -->
- **202** | already protected under State or federal endangered species laws <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 876-878; p.19 Table 1 line 999 -->
- **240** | identified as at risk by species experts, beyond the official lists <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 879-880 -->
- **163** | could be lost within five years without immediate emergency action <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 884-886; p.19 Table 1 line 999 -->
- **299** | have recovery goals and recovery footprints (88 interim, 211 full) <!-- src: plan p.19, Table 1 line 999 (88 + 211) -->
- **143** | need surveys or research before recovery goals can be set <!-- src: plan p.19, Table 1 line 999 (82 area surveys + 61 species-specific surveys) -->

## How at-risk species were identified
DOFAW coordinated taxon expert teams, called Taxonomic Teams, for each group of species. Working with other species experts, they reviewed species one by one. They drew on their own field knowledge, existing endangered species lists, Hawaiʻi's State Wildlife Action Plan, conservation databases, and the published literature. <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 866-869 -->

### The test: likely extinct within 20 years
A species was considered at risk if it is likely to become extinct within the next 20 years without significant management. <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 869-870 --> The main reasons species fall into this category are:

- very few individuals left
- steep declines in numbers or range caused by a changing climate
- newly introduced invasive species
- habitat loss
<!-- src: plan p.16, lines 870-873 -->

### Listed and not-yet-listed species
State and federal endangered species lists already included 202 of these species as endangered or threatened. <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 876-878 --> The other 240 were identified as at risk by the experts for each group. <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 879-880 -->

> **Callout:** More than half of Maui Nui's at-risk species (240 of 442) are not on any endangered species list. Planning only for listed species would leave them out. <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 874-880 -->

## Species facing imminent extinction
Of the 442 at-risk species, 163 are so close to extinction that they may be lost within the next five years without immediate emergency action. <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 884-886 --> They are:

- 102 Plants <!-- src: plan p.19, Table 1 line 994 -->
- all 49 Terrestrial snails <!-- src: plan p.19, Table 1 line 995; p.21 lines 1071-1073 -->
- 9 Terrestrial arthropods, all yellow-faced bees (*Hylaeus*) <!-- src: plan p.19, Table 1 line 996; p.64 lines 2667-2668 -->
- 2 Forest birds, the kiwikiu and the ʻākohekohe <!-- src: plan p.19, Table 1 line 991; p.33 Table 5 lines 1478-1481 -->
- 1 Aquatic invertebrate, the flying earwig Hawaiian damselfly (*Megalagrion nesiotes*) <!-- src: plan p.19, Table 1 line 997; p.71 Table 21 line 2892; App 3 line 6591 -->

No Seabirds, Waterbirds, or Hawaiian hoary bats are expected to be lost within five years. <!-- src: plan p.19, Table 1 lines 992-993, 998 -->

### What emergency action means
Emergency actions are chosen for how well and how quickly they work. They often happen outside the wild (ex situ), such as captive rearing and seed storage. Others are close to the wild, such as very small predator-proof exclosures. <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 888-890 --> Alongside these, the plan protects and manages key wild populations so that each species survives through the life of the plan. <!-- src: plan p.17, lines 896-899 -->

Most species that need emergency measures already have interim or full recovery goals. Several are still waiting on surveys. Whatever their category, every one of the 163 will receive actions aimed at preventing its extinction. <!-- src: plan p.16-17, lines 891-896 -->

[The emergency actions for each group are listed on the Using the Plan page](using-the-plan.html)

## Recovery of at-risk species
A key goal of the MNLCP is to restore at-risk species to healthy, self-sustaining populations. <!-- src: plan p.17, lines 902-903 --> Some species will still need ongoing management after they reach that point. The plan calls these "conservation-reliant" species and considers them no longer at risk of extinction once they meet their recovery targets. <!-- src: plan p.17, lines 903-907 -->

The MNLCP gives managers a flexible roadmap toward recovery for all 442 at-risk species. <!-- src: plan p.17, lines 909-910 --> Conservation works best when the people doing the work on the ground know what outcome they are aiming for, where the most important places are, and which actions to take first. <!-- src: plan p.17, lines 912-915 --> To support that, the plan:

1. maps recovery habitat (recovery footprints) wherever possible, and
2. identifies actions to address key threats and estimates how likely each is to help each group recover.
<!-- src: plan p.17, lines 917-921 -->

### Three kinds of recovery goal
Some at-risk species are well studied and some are barely known. So the plan sorts species into three categories, based on what is known about their biology and needs and whether proven management methods exist. <!-- src: plan p.17, lines 923-929 -->

**Full recovery goals** (211 species). These species have good information on their habitat needs, where that habitat is, their threats, and actions that work. Each has a population goal. Reaching it is expected to leave the species stable or increasing and no longer at risk, so it can come off the Maui Nui at-risk list. Many are federally and State listed and already have recovery plans or recovery guidance from the Hawaiʻi and Pacific Plant Recovery Coordinating Committee. <!-- src: plan p.18, lines 943-950; count p.19 Table 1 line 999 -->

**Interim recovery goals** (88 species). These species have enough information on habitat needs, threats, and actions, but something holds back full recovery. They may have few known places to manage, habitat may take a long time to restore, or the focus may have to stay on ex situ work. For example, there is not yet a way to control the predators of Terrestrial snails, so snails cannot yet be returned to the wild. Interim goals create viable populations and move conservation forward significantly, but more populations will be needed before the species is secure. <!-- src: plan p.17-18, lines 931-941; count p.19 Table 1 line 999 -->

**Insufficient data** (143 species). Some species lack the information needed to map their habitat needs. Many at-risk Terrestrial arthropods, for example, have fewer than five occurrence records. For these species, the MNLCP sets up surveys and research. Once the data are in hand, their habitat can be mapped and added to the plan. <!-- src: plan p.18, lines 953-961; count p.19 Table 1 line 999 --> Two kinds of survey are planned:

- **Area surveys** (82 species) for species whose host plants are known and can be mapped. These surveys target the planning units likely to hold many such species at once. They show where to look for species and habitat, not where to manage. <!-- src: plan p.18, lines 965-974; count p.19 Table 1 line 996 -->
- **Species-specific surveys** (61 species) for species that lack the information needed even for area surveys. The MNLCP does not map areas for these surveys. <!-- src: plan p.19, lines 977-979; count p.19 Table 1 line 999 -->

Both full and interim recovery species have recovery footprints showing where to place recovery populations. Species with insufficient data do not yet have recovery footprints. <!-- src: plan p.18, lines 941-942, 951-952, 961-963 -->

### Populations: how recovery goals are measured
Recovery goals are set as a number of healthy, self-sustaining populations. Examples from the plan:

- **Plants:** a desired number of recovery populations for each species, listed in Appendix 4. The full plant recovery footprint provides habitat for up to 10 populations of each of the 195 Plants with recovery goals. <!-- src: plan App 4 lines 7079-7334; p.28 lines 1294-1295 -->
- **Terrestrial arthropods:** an initial target of five healthy, self-sustaining populations for each species. <!-- src: plan p.64, lines 2693-2694 -->
- **Terrestrial snails:** an interim goal of three populations for each species, listed in Appendix 6. <!-- src: plan App 6 lines 7487-7541 -->

### Recovery footprints
A recovery footprint is the set of planning units that contain existing or restorable habitat a group needs to reach its recovery goals. <!-- src: plan p.21, lines 1063-1064 --> A planning unit (PU) is a mapping cell of about 2 km². <!-- src: plan p.vi, line 228 --> Recovery footprints answer the question of "where to focus" conservation. <!-- src: plan p.26, lines 1239-1241 --> Within them, priority footprints mark the most important areas to work first, and initial implementation footprints suggest where to start. <!-- src: plan p.26-27, lines 1242-1254 -->

Terrestrial snails are handled differently. The snail team found that all 49 at-risk snails face imminent extinction. Broad habitat recovery is not yet possible for them, so their conservation focuses first on preventing extinction in predator-proof snail exclosures (kīpuka kāhuli). Research on controlling invasive predators continues alongside this, to make future recovery possible. <!-- src: plan p.21, lines 1071-1079 -->

[Read how managers use footprints, actions, and timing together](using-the-plan.html)

:::details Why the plan's category counts add up to 442
Every at-risk species falls into exactly one category in Table 1: 88 with interim recovery goals, 211 with full recovery goals, 82 for area surveys, and 61 for species-specific surveys (88 + 211 + 82 + 61 = 442). <!-- src: plan p.19, Table 1 line 999 --> Facing imminent extinction is a separate flag that cuts across those categories. Most of the 163 species facing imminent extinction have recovery goals, but some Terrestrial arthropods facing imminent extinction are in the area survey category. <!-- src: plan p.16, lines 891-893; App 7 lines 7639-7655 -->

Two at-risk Plants, *Myoporum degeneri* and *Wollastonia populifolia*, do not yet have enough information to map recovery habitat. That is why 195 of the 197 Plants have recovery goals. <!-- src: plan App 4 lines 7240, 7332, 7343; p.19 Table 1 line 994 -->
:::

## Lead
Five Hawaiian waterbirds were likely native to Maui Nui, and all five are listed as endangered or threatened under federal and Hawaiʻi law. Two, the ʻalae ʻula and the kōloa maoli, are probably no longer found here. Waterbird conservation in Hawaiʻi has largely been successful, and the plan seeks to build on that success. The strategy centers on protecting and managing key wetlands (and uplands for nēnē), controlling introduced predators, and later returning the two missing species.

## Status and trends
In general, waterbirds have responded well to management. <!-- src: plan p.45, Ch 6c vi --> Along with Seabirds, they already have better than average persistence with current conservation efforts. <!-- src: plan p.40 --> Two species are missing. The ʻalae ʻula needs low-salinity freshwater wetlands, which are rare in Maui Nui. All ducks that resemble kōloa maoli appear to be hybrids with feral mallards. <!-- src: plan p.43 -->

## Threats
- **Wetland loss.** Human changes to wetlands have destroyed habitat. <!-- src: plan p.41 -->
- **Introduced mammalian predators.** Mongoose, cats, rats and domestic dogs. <!-- src: plan p.41 -->
- **Botulism.** Botulism poisoning is a local threat in some wetlands. <!-- src: plan p.41 -->
- **Hybridization.** Interbreeding between kōloa maoli and feral mallards is a highly significant threat. <!-- src: plan p.41 -->
- **Human-caused deaths.** For nēnē, the plan includes reducing human-caused mortality, such as car strikes. <!-- src: plan p.45; App 2 p.A2-35 -->

## Emergency actions
None of the waterbirds is facing imminent extinction, so no emergency actions are listed for this group. <!-- src: plan p.43, Ch 6c iii; p.21, Table 2 -->

## Recovery strategy

### Strategy by species
- **ʻAlae keʻokeʻo and ʻaeʻo.** Keep habitat suitable by managing water levels and flow, sediment and invasive weeds, and control predators, using predator-proof fencing where it is cost-effective. <!-- src: plan p.41 -->
- **Nēnē.** Control predators, restore upland areas and increase the population on Molokaʻi. <!-- src: plan p.41 -->
- **ʻAlae ʻula.** Protect and restore suitable habitat, then reintroduce the species to Molokaʻi. <!-- src: plan p.41 -->
- **Kōloa maoli.** First develop an effective way to remove the threat of hybridization. Once that works, it will be feasible to reintroduce kōloa maoli to Maui Nui. <!-- src: plan p.41 -->

The recovery footprint includes very important wetlands (and uplands for nēnē) as primary habitat for the populations needed to meet recovery requirements, plus supporting areas that provide additional habitat. <!-- src: plan p.43, Ch 6c iv -->

## Priority conservation actions
The plan's action prioritization used a modified Priority Threat Management (PTM) process. PTM estimates how much each of 27 strategies would raise the chance that each species group persists over 20 years. <!-- src: plan p.84, Ch 6j --> For ʻalae keʻokeʻo and ʻaeʻo, the most important actions are predator-proof fencing, wetland habitat protection and habitat management. For nēnē, they are restoration, predator control and reducing human-caused deaths. <!-- src: plan p.45 -->

- Waterbirds, like Seabirds and the Hawaiian hoary bat, do not depend on research or unproven future technologies to reach 50 percent persistence. <!-- src: plan p.86, Ch 6j -->
- The PTM waterbird group in Appendix 2 lists kōloa maoli, ʻalae keʻokeʻo, nēnē and ʻaeʻo. <!-- src: plan App 2 p.A2-19, Table 1 -->
- **What S10 includes for waterbirds:** build and maintain two new predator-proof fences per year for 10 years (20 fences in all) <!-- src: plan App 2 p.A2-33 -->, and new cat-proof fences where cats are the main predator or full predator-proof fences are hard to build. <!-- src: plan App 2 p.A2-33, Table 2 -->
- **What S6 includes:** removing invasive plants from wetlands; removing built-up sediment; planting forested bioshields around critical waterbird habitat; protecting future waterbird habitat from development and climate change through planning and zoning; pumping, diking and wells to manage water levels and salinity and reduce botulism outbreaks; and supporting restoration of loʻi with loʻi farmers. <!-- src: plan App 2 pp.A2-30 to A2-31, Table 2 -->
- **Waterbird parts of other strategies:** controlling cattle egret roosts in wetlands (S7); a network to find and remove bird carcasses and report avian botulism outbreaks (S14); power line diverters (S16); and speed bumps within Haleakalā and changes to roadside management to reduce nēnē car strikes (S16). <!-- src: plan App 2 pp.A2-31, A2-34, A2-35, Table 2 -->
:::

## Monitoring and research needs
- **Disease surveillance.** Continue surveillance, planning and response for avian influenza and other diseases. <!-- src: plan p.46 -->
- **Standard monitoring.** Standardize monitoring of waterbird populations at managed sites. <!-- src: plan p.46 -->
- **Hybridization research.** Develop methods to prevent kōloa maoli from hybridizing with feral mallards. Reintroduction cannot happen until these methods reduce hybridization to an acceptable level. <!-- src: plan pp.45-46 -->
- **Reintroduction sites.** Create and maintain good habitat as potential reintroduction sites for ʻalae ʻula. <!-- src: plan p.45 -->

## Island by island
- **Maui.** Home to most of today's coots (320), stilts (499) and nēnē (580). <!-- src: plan p.43, Table 14 --> The nēnē target here is two populations totaling 800 birds (600 and 200). <!-- src: plan p.44, Table 15 -->
- **Molokaʻi.** Has 30 coots, 142 stilts and 30 nēnē today. <!-- src: plan p.43, Table 14 --> The plan aims to increase nēnē here to 200 and to reintroduce ʻalae ʻula (250 birds). <!-- src: plan p.41; p.44, Table 15 --> The kōloa maoli target also lists 250 birds for Molokaʻi. <!-- src: plan p.44, Table 15 -->
- **Lānaʻi.** Has 14 coots and 43 stilts today, and can contribute to the Maui Nui-wide coot, stilt and kōloa maoli targets. <!-- src: plan pp.43-44, Tables 14-15 -->
- **Kahoʻolawe.** No waterbirds today and no waterbird targets. <!-- src: plan pp.43-44, Tables 14-15 -->

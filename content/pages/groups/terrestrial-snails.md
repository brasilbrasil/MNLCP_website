## Lead
Hawaiʻi once supported at least 750 native land snail species, and only 10 to 35 percent may still survive. <!-- src: plan p.57, lines 2398-2400 (citing Cowie et al. 2026) --> On Maui Nui, all 49 at-risk snail species could be lost within the next five years. <!-- src: plan p.57, lines 2403-2404 --> The main cause is an introduced predator, the rosy wolfsnail (*Euglandina rosea*). The plan therefore puts preventing extinction first: it protects snails behind predator-proof fences and in a laboratory until there are ways to control predators across the landscape.

## Why snails are in crisis
The situation for native land snails, kāhuli, is dire. <!-- src: plan p.57, lines 2396-2397 --> Hawaiʻi once supported at least 750 native land snail species, and only 10 to 35 percent may still survive. <!-- src: plan p.57, lines 2398-2400 --> Many of those that remain are at risk of extinction.

The Snail Taxonomic Team determined that all 49 at-risk snail species on Maui Nui face imminent extinction. <!-- src: plan p.21, lines 1071-1072 --> Because of this, the plan focuses first on preventing these losses through a network of exclosures. Broader habitat recovery is not possible yet. <!-- src: plan p.21, lines 1072-1079 -->

> **Callout:** Without extinction prevention there will be no snails to recover. Keeping three populations of every species safe is the plan's interim recovery step. <!-- src: plan p.60-61, lines 2535-2544 -->

## The species
The 49 at-risk snails belong to nine genera. More than half (29) are in the genus *Partulina*. Others include *Newcombia*, *Perdicella*, *Laminella*, *Pleuropoma*, *Amastra*, *Leptachatina*, *Auriculella* and *Lyropupa*. <!-- src: plan App 3 (Terrestrial Snails rows) and App 6 pp.A6-1 to A6-2, lines 7482-7553 --> Many survive in only one or two known wild populations, and two have none known in the wild. <!-- src: plan App 6, Current Populations column -->

Three species are listed as endangered: Newcomb's tree snail (*Newcombia cumingi*) and two Lānaʻi tree snails (*Partulina semicarinata* and *Partulina variabilis*). <!-- src: plan App 6 (ESA Listing column); App 3 common names --> The other 46 are not listed, but experts found them just as close to extinction. This is a clear example of why the plan looks beyond the endangered species list.

Experts worked with Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) rather than formal species names, because some snails are taxonomically uncertain or are species complexes. The plan reports them as species. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-7, lines 5196-5198 -->

## Status and trends
Snails are declining fast statewide, and safe habitat outside exclosures keeps shrinking. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-6, lines 5188-5190 --> Occurrence data are sparse for many species, so the plan calls for field surveys to find remaining wild populations. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-7, lines 5225-5226; p.59 lines 2486-2487 -->

## Threats
- **Rosy wolfsnail (*Euglandina rosea*).** This introduced predatory snail is the most important threat. <!-- src: plan p.57, lines 2404-2406; p.86 lines 3445-3448 --> There are no proven methods yet to control it across a landscape. <!-- src: plan p.86, lines 3448-3450 -->
- **Other introduced predators.** These include the predatory snail *Oxychilus alliarius*, Jackson's chameleon and rats. <!-- src: plan p.57, lines 2418-2421 and 2423-2425 -->
- **Habitat threats at future recovery sites.** When habitat restoration begins, recovery sites will also need fire management, biosecurity and infrastructure, and removal of key weeds such as strawberry guava. <!-- src: plan p.57-58, lines 2428-2432; p.61 lines 2562-2564 -->

> **Callout:** Like forest birds and avian malaria, snails face one overwhelming threat. Until rosy wolfsnail can be controlled across the landscape, snail conservation relies on laboratories and on the quasi-in situ kīpuka kāhuli exclosures. <!-- src: plan p.86, lines 3445-3453 -->

## Recovery strategy
The strategy's long-term aim is to restore every surviving species to healthy, self-sustaining populations in the wild. <!-- src: plan p.57, lines 2400-2402 --> For now, the focus is on preventing extinction.

### Goals
- **Interim recovery goal:** keep three populations of each of the 49 species in predator-proof exclosures (kīpuka kāhuli), and in laboratory rearing facilities if needed. <!-- src: plan p.60-61, lines 2538-2544 -->
- **Next step:** expand populations outside kīpuka kāhuli. <!-- src: plan p.61, lines 2544-2545 -->
- **Deferred:** landscape-level habitat management to recover snails and remove them from the at-risk list. This waits until there are workable ways to control rosy wolfsnail. <!-- src: plan p.62, lines 2588-2590 -->

### Emergency actions to prevent extinction
The plan's snail objectives are: <!-- src: plan p.58-59, lines 2461-2487 -->
- Rescue populations that could be eliminated within five years by rosy wolfsnail, rats or chameleons. Move them into laboratory captive rearing and/or kīpuka kāhuli.
- Build a network of 32 kīpuka kāhuli that protects at least three populations of each species. Build first where the most at-risk species are, and place sites across Maui Nui's climates so the network can hold all 49 species cost-effectively.
- Establish an ex situ captive rearing program and laboratory on Maui that can house all 49 species. <!-- src: plan p.57 lines 2412-2413; p.59 line 2480 -->
- Research ways to control invasive predators, especially rosy wolfsnail, across the landscape.
- Control predators at wild populations until snails can be moved somewhere secure.
- Survey for more populations of at-risk species, and for areas without rosy wolfsnail to guide search-and-rescue work.

DOFAW staff and Hawaiʻi's Snail Extinction Prevention Program already do this work. The program played a central role in developing the plan's snail strategy, and the plan will greatly expand these efforts. <!-- src: plan p.58-59, lines 2458-2460 and 2491-2495 -->

### The kīpuka kāhuli network (preventing extinction footprint)
A kīpuka kāhuli is a small, managed refuge enclosed by a predator-proof fence that keeps out invasive predators. <!-- src: plan p.58, lines 2467-2468; App 1 p.A1-6 lines 5187-5188 --> Unlike other groups, the snail footprint is a network of exclosure sites, not a recovery footprint. Its job is to prevent extinction while keeping options open for future recovery planning. <!-- src: plan App 1 pp.A1-6 to A1-7, lines 5189-5193 -->

The network was developed in three steps: <!-- src: plan p.59, lines 2501-2517 -->
1. **Finding potential sites.** Snails followed the plan's algorithmic pathway, using the OptimTFE prioritization algorithm. Candidate sites were judged on elevation, slope, ruggedness, native habitat status, fire risk, climate resilience and habitat structure. The goal was a set of viable sites that could protect at least three populations of each species.
2. **Selecting sites.** After extensive review by snail experts, the final network has 32 exclosures that meet each species' habitat needs: 4 to 6 already planned for construction, and 26 to be built in the future.
3. **Setting priorities.** A priority network focuses the first construction on species now found only in captivity and those closest to extinction. Appendix 1 gives it as 14 sites that together hold one population of each of the 49 species. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-7, lines 5214-5216 -->

Many exclosures are close to known snail occurrences. For 39 of the 49 species, the nearest known occurrence is less than 2 km from an exclosure. <!-- src: plan p.61, lines 2551-2553 --> The exclosures may later serve as protected bases for returning snails to their historic locations. <!-- src: plan p.61, lines 2548-2551 -->

[figure: Map of the full 32-site kīpuka kāhuli network, with yellow priority sites (one population of each species) and green sites (completing three per species); second panel shows red sites for initial implementation | suggested source: plan PDF p.60 Figure 11 (a) and (b) | alt: Map of Maui Nui showing the 32 planned predator-proof snail exclosure sites, colored by priority]

:::details How the snail network was prioritized (practitioner detail)
- **Pathway.** Terrestrial snails were prioritized algorithmically with the OptimTFE R package within the Transparent, Flexible, Expert-engaged Systematic Conservation Planning (TFE-SCP) process. The planning units were candidate exclosure sites screened for biological suitability and constructability, not the standard grid of roughly 2 km² planning units used for other groups. Existing and planned exclosures were carried forward. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-7, lines 5204-5206; p.A1-5 lines 5150-5152 -->
- **Species units and habitat.** Focal OTUs came from the Snail Extinction Prevention Program and expert-provided lists. Most taxa had too few records for conventional species distribution models, so habitat compatibility was estimated with expert-reviewed climate-similarity models. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-7, lines 5196-5203 -->
- **Targets and rules.** Each OTU has three targets within biodiversity reserves and its historical range. There are limits on the number of OTUs per exclosure and constraints against putting incompatible taxa together. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-7, lines 5206-5208 -->
- **Criteria.** The snail team emphasized exclosure count, accessibility, fire risk and climate resilience. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-7, lines 5208-5209 -->
- **Expert fine-tuning.** Experts refined the draft network in the Adaptive Conservation Planner (ACP), resolving species incompatibilities, packing, climate fit and access constraints. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-7, lines 5209-5211 -->
- **Using it.** For each site, the Conservation Planner shows the targeted OTUs, their habitat compatibility scores, occurrence evidence and expert notes. The priority network shows where to start building so that every species has at least one protected population. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-7, lines 5218-5222 -->
- **Limitations.** The network reflects the best information available and should be updated as surveys find more populations. It is designed to prevent extinction and enable future recovery. It does not address landscape-scale threats. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-7, lines 5224-5229 -->
:::

## Priority conservation actions
The plan's action prioritization (a modified Priority Threat Management, or PTM, analysis, led by UH Mānoa) estimated how much each strategy would raise the chance that snails persist for the plan's 20-year timeframe. <!-- src: plan p.61, lines 2555-2559; p.84 lines 3314-3322; Exec Summary p.v line 151 --> Snail survival depends heavily on countering one threat, rosy wolfsnail predation. If effective control methods are found and used, the chance that snails persist rises above 50 percent. <!-- src: plan p.61, lines 2557-2561 -->

[table: Suggested sequencing of Strategy 25 actions to benefit snails (plan Table 18). The plan's caption says that if all actions are implemented, snails could have up to a 63 percent chance of persisting through the plan's 20-year timeframe. <!-- src: plan p.61, Table 18, lines 2565-2579 -->]
| Action | Description | Persistence (%) | Sequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| S10 | New predator fencing (snails, rodents, mongoose, cats) | 44.0 | 1 |
| S13 | Future tools for rare plants and invertebrates (develop ex situ methods, establish invertebrate and plant propagation facilities, develop controls for coconut rhinoceros beetles, black twig borers and naio thrips) | 36.9 | 2 |
| S8 | Invasive invertebrate predator control (ants and yellow jackets) | 35.0 | 4 |
| S11 | Landscape-scale rat suppression | 31.9 | 6 |
| S7 | Invasive vertebrate predator control (rats, cats, dogs, mongoose, barn owl, cattle egrets, rabbits) | 31.0 | 5 |
| S12 | Existing tools for rare plants and invertebrates | 30.5 | 3 |

The plan's suggested order of actions: <!-- src: plan p.62, lines 2592-2612 -->

**Near term**
1. New predator fencing for snails.
2. Future tools for rare plants and invertebrates. This especially means better captive rearing and landscape-level control methods for rosy wolfsnail, *Oxychilus alliarius*, chameleons and rats. It also means building and maintaining databases on wild and captive populations.
3. Existing tools for rare plants and invertebrates.

**Future**

4. Landscape control of invasive invertebrate predators (rosy wolfsnail and others).
5. Local control of invasive vertebrate predators (rats and chameleons).
6. Landscape-scale rat suppression.

> **Callout:** New fencing and new tools come first. Across all groups, the plan names landscape-scale control of rosy wolfsnail as one of the new tools snails depend on most. <!-- src: plan Exec Summary p.vii, lines 268-270 -->

:::details Persistence figures elsewhere in the plan, and a caution on predator control

Current actions give 26 percent and no action 14 percent. <!-- src: plan p.85 Table 26 line 3410; App 2 Table 1 p.A2-15 line 5678 --> Appendix 2 adds that persistence comes close to 50 percent under combinations that include new predator exclusion fences, invasive invertebrate pest control and current snail management (Strategies 23, 24 and 27). <!-- src: plan App 2 p.A2-10, lines 5574-5578 -->

## Surveys and data gaps
- Survey for more populations of at-risk species. <!-- src: plan p.59, line 2486 -->
- Survey for areas without rosy wolfsnail, to support search-and-rescue work that secures strong genetic representation of each species. <!-- src: plan p.57 lines 2414-2416; p.59 line 2487 -->
- Identify recovery habitat for each of the 49 species, plus the landing zones and staging campsites needed to reach it. <!-- src: plan p.57, lines 2428-2431 -->
- Update the exclosure network as surveys find more populations. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-7, lines 5225-5226 -->

## Monitoring and research needs
- **Landscape-level control of key threats:** rosy wolfsnail, Jackson's chameleon and rats. <!-- src: plan p.57, lines 2423-2425 -->
- **Ex situ methods:** ways to store genetic material and to propagate all at-risk snail species. <!-- src: plan p.57, line 2426 -->
- **Population records:** databases on wild and captive populations. <!-- src: plan p.62, lines 2598-2600 -->
- **Infrastructure:** the Maui laboratory and propagation capacity under S13. <!-- src: plan p.57 lines 2412-2413; App 2 S13 item 6, p.A2-34, lines 6406-6408 -->

## How this fits the whole plan
The 49 snails have sites identified for kīpuka kāhuli but no mapped recovery habitat yet, so the plan's combined recovery footprint figures count them separately. <!-- src: plan p.81, lines 3245-3250 --> Snail exclosure sites can be viewed with all other group footprints in the Conservation Planner. <!-- src: plan App 1 p.A1-5, lines 5132-5135 -->

## Lead
Maui Nui is home to an extraordinary concentration of species found nowhere else on Earth, and to one of the most severe extinction crises in the nation. <!-- src: plan p.iv, lines 106-107 --> The Maui Nui Landscape Conservation Plan (MNLCP), led by the Hawaiʻi Division of Forestry and Wildlife (DOFAW), shows where to act, what to do, and when, so that the needs of all at-risk species are met as efficiently as possible. <!-- src: plan p.iv, lines 107-111, 131-134 -->

## Key numbers
- **442** | native species at risk of extinction across Maui Nui <!-- src: plan p.iv, lines 113-116; Table 1 p.v-vi -->
- **163** | could be lost within five years without emergency action <!-- src: plan p.iv, lines 113-116; p.v lines 165-166 -->
- **240** | at-risk species not yet on state or federal endangered species lists <!-- src: plan p.iv, lines 113-116; p.v lines 163-164 -->
- **100+** | land managers, scientists, and species experts contributed their expertise <!-- src: plan p.iv, lines 113-116 -->
- **4** | islands: Maui, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, and Kahoʻolawe <!-- src: plan p.iv, lines 110-111 -->
- **20** | year implementation period <!-- src: plan p.v, lines 154-155 -->

## The challenge
With less than 0.2 percent <!-- src: plan p.iv, line 122 --> of the nation's land area, Hawaiʻi accounts for 29 percent <!-- src: plan p.iv, lines 122-123 (USFWS 2026) --> of its imperiled species. More than 60 percent <!-- src: plan p.iv, lines 123-124 --> of Hawaiʻi's native ecosystems have been destroyed or highly degraded. Many native species now survive only in small, scattered populations in high-elevation refuges or fenced areas.

Invasive predators, ungulates (hoofed animals such as goats, deer, and cattle), weeds, disease, fire, and climate change continue to drive declines. <!-- src: plan p.iv, lines 125-126; ungulate examples p.4-5 lines 488-491 -->

Many good plans already exist, but they are not integrated at the landscape or management level. Managers must reconcile dozens of documents with different assumptions and scales while working with limited budgets and staff. <!-- src: plan p.iv, lines 127-131 -->

> **Callout:** The question is not only that 442 species are at risk. It is where each species should be recovered, what actions will do the most good, and when they must happen. <!-- src: plan p.iv, lines 131-133 -->

## Three integrated goals
1. **Prevent extinctions** of the most imperiled native plants and animals.
2. **Recover all at-risk species** to healthy, self-sustaining population levels.
3. **Protect and restore native ecosystems** so they provide habitat for all Maui Nui biodiversity and sustain clean water, cultural values, and local economies.

## Who, where, what, and when
The MNLCP uses an Integrated Multi-Scale Conservation Planning (IMCP) approach. It links several analyses into one decision-support system, so managers can answer four practical questions. <!-- src: plan p.v, lines 149-154; p.29 lines 1354-1360 -->

- **Who needs help?** The at-risk species list, built with seven expert Taxonomic Teams. <!-- src: plan p.v lines 152-153; p.29 lines 1355-1356 -->
- **Where to manage?** Spatial prioritization maps the conservation footprints where recovery can happen. This work was led by USGS. <!-- src: plan p.v line 150 -->
- **What to do?** Action prioritization, through a modified Priority Threat Management (PTM) process, weighs the benefit, feasibility, and cost of conservation strategies. This work was led by UH Mānoa. <!-- src: plan p.v lines 151-152; p.vii lines 251-253 -->
- **When to do it?** DOFAW land managers turned the results into implementation guidance: emergency actions first, then a recommended sequence of recovery actions. <!-- src: plan p.v lines 153-154; p.viii lines 286-294 -->

Ecosystem prioritization, led by UH Hilo, adds the wider landscape. It identifies where protecting and restoring native ecosystems will benefit all of Maui Nui's plants and animals. <!-- src: plan p.v line 152; p.viii lines 299-300; p.9 lines 638-639 -->

## Who needs help: 442 at-risk species
Experts identified 442 <!-- src: plan p.v line 162 --> native species at risk of extinction within the next 20 years without significant intervention. Only 202 <!-- src: plan p.v line 163 --> are protected under state or federal endangered species laws. The other 240 <!-- src: plan p.v line 164 --> were identified by species experts because of very small or declining populations, restricted ranges, or climate vulnerability. Of the 442, 163 <!-- src: plan p.v line 165 --> could be lost within five years without immediate emergency action.

## Where to act: conservation footprints
For each taxonomic group, the plan maps the planning units (PUs, areas of about 2 km² each) <!-- src: plan p.vi, line 228 --> that contain the existing or restorable habitat needed to reach recovery goals. These maps are called recovery footprints. Combined across all groups: <!-- src: plan p.vi, lines 228-230 -->

- The full **recovery footprint** covers 492 <!-- src: plan p.vi, line 237 --> planning units and provides habitat for 299 <!-- src: plan p.vi, lines 237-238; see notes (Ch 6i p.81 line 3246 gives 250) --> at-risk species.
- The **priority footprint** narrows this to 216 <!-- src: plan p.vi, line 239; see notes (release candidate 237) --> planning units, about 43 percent <!-- src: plan p.vi, line 239 --> of the full footprint, while still supporting habitat for 267 <!-- src: plan p.vi, line 240; see notes (release candidate 305 taxa) --> at-risk species. These are the most important places to start.
- About 66 percent <!-- src: plan p.vi, line 242 --> of the footprint is already on conservation-oriented land, and about half <!-- src: plan p.vi, line 243 --> already has some ungulate fencing.
- The footprints span all four islands, centered on upland Haleakalā, West Maui, East Molokaʻi, and Lānaʻihale, and generally have above-average climate resilience. <!-- src: plan p.vi-vii, lines 243-246 -->
- 143 <!-- src: plan p.vii, line 247 --> species, mostly Terrestrial arthropods, still lack the data needed to map recovery habitat. The plan sets out targeted surveys so these species can be added over time.

## What to do: priority conservation actions
Working with about 100 <!-- src: plan p.vii, line 251 --> managers and scientists, the Action Prioritization Team evaluated the expected benefit, feasibility, and cost of 27 <!-- src: plan p.vii, line 252 --> conservation strategies (17 single-focus strategies and 10 combinations) <!-- src: plan p.vii, lines 252-253 --> for 17 <!-- src: plan p.vii, line 253 --> species groups. The target is at least a 50 percent <!-- src: plan p.vii, line 254 --> chance that each group persists with viable, self-sustaining populations over 20 years.

- **No single strategy is enough.** Two complementary combination strategies, S25 (plant- and invertebrate-focused) and S27 (bird- and bat-focused), come closest to meeting every group's needs when implemented together. <!-- src: plan p.vii, lines 256-258 -->
- **Doing nothing, or only what is done now, is not enough.** Current efforts help, especially for Seabirds and Waterbirds, but feasible new actions improve outcomes for every group. <!-- src: plan p.vii, lines 259-261 -->
- **Foundational habitat work pays off.** Ungulate fencing and removal, weed control, predator control, and wetland management benefit many species at once. <!-- src: plan p.vii, lines 262-265 -->
- **New tools are the most important investment.** Future tools for rare plant and invertebrate conservation (S13) ranked in the top three actions for 13 of the 17 <!-- src: plan p.vii, lines 266-268 --> species groups.
- **Some groups are within reach with proven methods.** Seabirds, Waterbirds, and the Hawaiian hoary bat can reach the 50 percent goal without unproven technologies. Plants remain the hardest group to secure. <!-- src: plan p.vii, lines 271-274 -->

## When to act: emergency first, then sequenced recovery
The 163 <!-- src: plan p.viii, line 286 --> species facing imminent extinction need immediate emergency measures: seed banking and captive propagation, very small predator-proof exclosures, and intensive protection of the last wild populations. Examples include emergency propagation for 102 <!-- src: plan p.viii, line 289 --> plants, a network of kīpuka kāhuli (snail exclosures) and an ex situ laboratory for all 49 <!-- src: plan p.viii, lines 289-290 --> at-risk snails, and protection of the only known Maui Nui population of the damselfly *Megalagrion nesiotes*. <!-- src: plan p.viii, lines 286-291 -->

Beyond emergencies, Taxonomic Teams recommended an implementation sequence for each group. Start with the highest-benefit actions and the foundational steps (fencing, ungulate removal, weed control) that set the stage for later reintroductions. Pursue cost-effective, field-ready actions as opportunities arise. <!-- src: plan p.viii, lines 291-294 -->

## A living plan
The MNLCP is designed for a 20-year <!-- src: plan p.v, line 154 --> implementation period and is delivered as a living system. It includes the Adaptive Conservation Planner, a spatial tool managers can use to build annual work plans and update priorities as conditions, knowledge, and funding change. <!-- src: plan p.v, lines 154-157 --> Monitoring and adaptive management track progress over time. <!-- src: plan p.ix, lines 332-334 -->

## What success requires
The MNLCP treats Maui Nui's biodiversity crisis not as an insurmountable problem but as a finite, solvable challenge. Realizing it will require leadership and partners to: <!-- src: plan p.ix, lines 318-319 -->

- Fund emergency actions now for the 163 <!-- src: plan p.ix, line 320 --> species that could be lost within five years.
- Invest in new tools for landscape-scale control of mosquitoes, predatory snails, rats, and other key threats, and for captive rearing and propagation of at-risk plants and invertebrates.
- Sustain and expand foundational management in the recovery footprint: fencing, ungulate removal, weed and predator control, wetland and instream-flow management, and fire prevention.
- Build partnerships with private landowners, communities, and agencies.
- Protect, manage, and restore native ecosystems.
- Fill data gaps through targeted surveys for the 143 <!-- src: plan p.ix, line 331 --> species that cannot yet be mapped.
- Use the plan as a living system, with the Conservation Planner guiding annual work plans and budgets.
<!-- src: plan p.ix, lines 320-334 -->

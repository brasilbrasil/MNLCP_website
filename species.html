    At-Risk Species

    The first step in conservation planning is knowing which species are at risk. The Maui Nui Landscape Conservation Plan (MNLCP) identifies 442 native birds, plants, invertebrates, and a bat that are likely to go extinct within the next 20 years without significant management. For each one, the plan sets out what is needed to prevent its extinction and, wherever the information allows, where and how to recover it.

    On this page

      How at-risk species were identified

      DOFAW coordinated taxon expert teams, called Taxonomic Teams, for each group of species. Working with other species experts, they reviewed species one by one. They drew on their own field knowledge, existing endangered species lists, Hawaiʻi's State Wildlife Action Plan, conservation databases, and the published literature.plan p.16, lines 866-869

      The test: likely extinct within 20 years

      A species was considered at risk if it is likely to become extinct within the next 20 years without significant management.plan p.16, lines 869-870 The main reasons species fall into this category are:

      • very few individuals left
      • steep declines in numbers or range caused by a changing climate
      • newly introduced invasive species
      • habitat lossplan p.16, lines 870-873

      Listed and not-yet-listed species

      State and federal endangered species lists already included 202 of these species as endangered or threatened.plan p.16, lines 876-878 The other 240 were identified as at risk by the experts for each group.plan p.16, lines 879-880

      More than half of Maui Nui's at-risk species (240 of 442) are not on any endangered species list. Planning only for listed species would leave them out.plan p.16, lines 874-880

      Species facing imminent extinction

      Of the 442 at-risk species, 163 are so close to extinction that they may be lost within the next five years without immediate emergency action.plan p.16, lines 884-886 They are:

      • 102 Plantsplan p.19, Table 1 line 994
      • all 49 Terrestrial snailsplan p.19, Table 1 line 995; p.21 lines 1071-1073
      • 9 Terrestrial arthropods, all yellow-faced bees (Hylaeus)plan p.19, Table 1 line 996; p.64 lines 2667-2668
      • 2 Forest birds, the kiwikiu and the ʻākohekoheplan p.19, Table 1 line 991; p.33 Table 5 lines 1478-1481
      • 1 Aquatic invertebrate, the flying earwig Hawaiian damselfly (Megalagrion nesiotes)plan p.19, Table 1 line 997; p.71 Table 21 line 2892; App 3 line 6591

      No Seabirds, Waterbirds, or Hawaiian hoary bats are expected to be lost within five years.plan p.19, Table 1 lines 992-993, 998

      What emergency action means

      Emergency actions are chosen for how well and how quickly they work. They often happen outside the wild (ex situ), such as captive rearing and seed storage. Others are close to the wild, such as very small predator-proof exclosures.plan p.16, lines 888-890 Alongside these, the plan protects and manages key wild populations so that each species survives through the life of the plan.plan p.17, lines 896-899

      Most species that need emergency measures already have interim or full recovery goals. Several are still waiting on surveys. Whatever their category, every one of the 163 will receive actions aimed at preventing its extinction.plan p.16-17, lines 891-896

      The emergency actions for each group are listed on the Using the Plan page

      Recovery of at-risk species

      A key goal of the MNLCP is to restore at-risk species to healthy, self-sustaining populations.plan p.17, lines 902-903 Some species will still need ongoing management after they reach that point. The plan calls these "conservation-reliant" species and considers them no longer at risk of extinction once they meet their recovery targets.plan p.17, lines 903-907

      The MNLCP gives managers a flexible roadmap toward recovery for all 442 at-risk species.plan p.17, lines 909-910 Conservation works best when the people doing the work on the ground know what outcome they are aiming for, where the most important places are, and which actions to take first.plan p.17, lines 912-915 To support that, the plan:

      1. maps recovery habitat (recovery footprints) wherever possible, and
      2. identifies actions to address key threats and estimates how likely each is to help each group recover.plan p.17, lines 917-921

      Three kinds of recovery goal

      Some at-risk species are well studied and some are barely known. So the plan sorts species into three categories, based on what is known about their biology and needs and whether proven management methods exist.plan p.17, lines 923-929

      Full recovery goals (211 species). These species have good information on their habitat needs, where that habitat is, their threats, and actions that work. Each has a population goal. Reaching it is expected to leave the species stable or increasing and no longer at risk, so it can come off the Maui Nui at-risk list. Many are federally and State listed and already have recovery plans or recovery guidance from the Hawaiʻi and Pacific Plant Recovery Coordinating Committee.plan p.18, lines 943-950; count p.19 Table 1 line 999

      Interim recovery goals (88 species). These species have enough information on habitat needs, threats, and actions, but something holds back full recovery. They may have few known places to manage, habitat may take a long time to restore, or the focus may have to stay on ex situ work. For example, there is not yet a way to control the predators of Terrestrial snails, so snails cannot yet be returned to the wild. Interim goals create viable populations and move conservation forward significantly, but more populations will be needed before the species is secure.plan p.17-18, lines 931-941; count p.19 Table 1 line 999

      Insufficient data (143 species). Some species lack the information needed to map their habitat needs. Many at-risk Terrestrial arthropods, for example, have fewer than five occurrence records. For these species, the MNLCP sets up surveys and research. Once the data are in hand, their habitat can be mapped and added to the plan.plan p.18, lines 953-961; count p.19 Table 1 line 999 Two kinds of survey are planned:

      • Area surveys (82 species) for species whose host plants are known and can be mapped. These surveys target the planning units likely to hold many such species at once. They show where to look for species and habitat, not where to manage.plan p.18, lines 965-974; count p.19 Table 1 line 996
      • Species-specific surveys (61 species) for species that lack the information needed even for area surveys. The MNLCP does not map areas for these surveys.plan p.19, lines 977-979; count p.19 Table 1 line 999

      Both full and interim recovery species have recovery footprints showing where to place recovery populations. Species with insufficient data do not yet have recovery footprints.plan p.18, lines 941-942, 951-952, 961-963

      Populations: how recovery goals are measured

      Recovery goals are set as a number of healthy, self-sustaining populations. Examples from the plan:

      • Plants: a desired number of recovery populations for each species, listed in Appendix 4. The full plant recovery footprint provides habitat for up to 10 populations of each of the 195 Plants with recovery goals.plan App 4 lines 7079-7334; p.28 lines 1294-1295
      • Terrestrial arthropods: an initial target of five healthy, self-sustaining populations for each species.plan p.64, lines 2693-2694
      • Terrestrial snails: an interim goal of three populations for each species, listed in Appendix 6.plan App 6 lines 7487-7541

      Recovery footprints

      A recovery footprint is the set of planning units that contain existing or restorable habitat a group needs to reach its recovery goals.plan p.21, lines 1063-1064 A planning unit (PU) is a mapping cell of about 2 km².plan p.vi, line 228 Recovery footprints answer the question of "where to focus" conservation.plan p.26, lines 1239-1241 Within them, priority footprints mark the most important areas to work first, and initial implementation footprints suggest where to start.plan p.26-27, lines 1242-1254

      Terrestrial snails are handled differently. The snail team found that all 49 at-risk snails face imminent extinction. Broad habitat recovery is not yet possible for them, so their conservation focuses first on preventing extinction in predator-proof snail exclosures (kīpuka kāhuli). Research on controlling invasive predators continues alongside this, to make future recovery possible.plan p.21, lines 1071-1079

      Why the plan's category counts add up to 442Details

      Every at-risk species falls into exactly one category in Table 1: 88 with interim recovery goals, 211 with full recovery goals, 82 for area surveys, and 61 for species-specific surveys (88 + 211 + 82 + 61 = 442).plan p.19, Table 1 line 999 Facing imminent extinction is a separate flag that cuts across those categories. Most of the 163 species facing imminent extinction have recovery goals, but some Terrestrial arthropods facing imminent extinction are in the area survey category.plan p.16, lines 891-893; App 7 lines 7639-7655

      Two at-risk Plants, Myoporum degeneri and Wollastonia populifolia, do not yet have enough information to map recovery habitat. That is why 195 of the 197 Plants have recovery goals.plan App 4 lines 7240, 7332, 7343; p.19 Table 1 line 994

      Explore the species

      The species explorer below lists all 442 at-risk species in the plan. You can search by scientific, common, or Hawaiian name and filter by group, listing status, imminent extinction, and recovery category. For Plants, Terrestrial snails, Terrestrial arthropods, and Aquatic invertebrates it also shows the plan's figures for each species, such as known occupied planning units and the recovery goal.plan App 3 lines 6588-7078; App 4 lines 7079-7348; App 6 lines 7482-7553; App 7 lines 7554-7774; Table 21 lines 2875-2899

      Names and figures appear as printed in the plan's appendices. Some entries are groups of related taxa or species not yet formally described, such as "Partulina mighelsiana ssp." or "Philodoria sp.". The experts counted each of these as its own at-risk entry.plan App 3 lines 6971-6972, 7038

      About the species dataDetails

      The explorer is built from the plan's Appendix 3 (the full at-risk list) together with Appendix 4 (Plants), Appendix 6 (Terrestrial snails), Appendix 7 (Terrestrial arthropods), and the species tables in each taxonomic group chapter (Forest birds, Seabirds, Waterbirds, Aquatic invertebrates, Hawaiian hoary bat).plan App 3, 4, 6, 7; Ch 6 Tables 5, 9, 13, 21, 23

      • Plants facing imminent extinction are the Plants in the Plant Extinction Prevention Program (PEPP). Appendix 4 also flags species with few populations (POP) and species that are rare on an island (ROI).plan p.27, lines 1264-1266; App 4 lines 7340-7342
      • Island-by-island distribution is not part of the plan's species appendices, so the explorer does not yet filter by island.
      • Hawaiian names for Plants are shown as they appear in Appendix 3. They have not yet had a Hawaiian-language review.

      Maui Nui Landscape Conservation Plan

      A landscape-scale roadmap to prevent extinctions, recover at-risk species and restore native ecosystems across Maui, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi and Kahoʻolawe. Led by the Hawaiʻi Division of Forestry and Wildlife.
